Reading Response

01

Reflection

I often associate futuristic design with unfamiliar technology or dramatic changes in how people live. However, from this reading, the author suggests that a convincing future grows from the relationships, habits, and contradictions already present around us. It actually somewhat feels like thinking through the past. This makes imagining the future feel more connected to observing the present carefully.

I was particularly interested in the idea of “thick” futurescapes. The authors explain that a believable future needs specific cultural and social details. I often think the future would be very differen, but acutually imagining a new device also involves asking who uses it, who maintains it, how it enters everyday routines, and what relationships it changes. This is just the same design process that we are doing right now. These questions made me realize how easily a speculative project can focus on an exciting invention while leaving its consequences underdeveloped. An ordinary detail, such as an advertisement or a household object, might reveal more about a future society than a broad explanation of its technology.

The discussion of montage also stood out to me. Combining fragments of the past, present, and future challenges the expectation that innovation requires starting from nothing. I find this approach useful because it allows familiar objects and practices to take on unfamiliar meanings. This actually inspired me a lot for my time capsule project, and I tried to see familiar things from a different perspective like I never knew it before. A future could contain advanced technologies alongside old buildings, inherited traditions, and outdated systems. Their coexistence creates tensions that make the scenario more complex. It also raises a question for me: whose lives change quickly, and whose remain shaped by older conditions?

I agreed with the author that we should not see the future as entirely utopian or dystopian. Both extremes can communicate a clear message, but they can also make a scenario feel predetermined. Everyday life usually contains benefits and problems at the same time. And thinking of what we have right now, our life could also feel utopian for people from the past. A technology might increase convenience while creating dependence, or help one community while placing additional burdens on another. I think leaving these contradictions visible gives an audience more room to form their own interpretations.
