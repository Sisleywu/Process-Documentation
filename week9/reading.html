Week 09 — Ideation & Prototyping

Reading Reflection

Reflection

“Design Futurescaping” talked about research in art and design, I think the differentiation between research into, for and through is an interesting idea. I think these approaches are highly related to ideation and prototyping, that we are using all of these research. When we are brainstorming about an an idea, we are researching into it; when we are prototyping, we are researching through art and design; when are reflecting our processes, we are research for our artistic expression and later design. This connects to moments when I discover a problem only after trying to build something. A material might behave unexpectedly, or someone might interpret an interaction differently from what I intended. These experiences can reshape the question I began with. At the same time, Frayling's distinctions remind me that making something does not automatically make it research. I need to identify what I learned and find a way to communicate it beyond the finished object.

The lifecycle framework in Making and Being could also connect to the research. Its phases include support, sourcing, labor, representation, maintenance, and departure. I usually give more attention to what a project communicates than to the conditions that allow me to produce it. I realized through this reading that I actually had assumptions made before an artwork is created. In fact, even an individually made project relies on other people, shared facilities, and forms of care. A detailed scenario can remain limited by its designer's experiences. Inviting people to contribute through drawing, building, or storytelling could introduce concerns that the designer would otherwise overlook.

The departure phase also made me think differently about my pangolin sculpture, which used old clothes, packaging, receipts, and plastic bags. I chose those materials because they represented my consumption and connected to the commodification of animals. However, turning waste into an artwork does not resolve the question of where those materials eventually go. Can the sculpture be taken apart? Can its components be reused, or have I combined them in ways that make that harder? The reading encourages me to consider these questions while designing, rather than after an exhibition ends.

“A City Is Not a Computer” also brings an interesting idea that documentation can reveal discoveries, but it can also exclude uncertainty. A finished artwork can communicate an idea while concealing the labor and resources behind it. For future projects, I want to document decisions and unresolved questions alongside successful outcomes, and consider maintenance and reuse earlier.
